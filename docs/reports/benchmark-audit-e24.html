True Corp · Retention labelling · Experiment 24

The benchmark was wrong, not the models

We stopped to validate the eval before changing anything. Three frontier models, reading blind, disagreed with our ground truth on 63% of disputed cases — and the defect they found was in the corpus generator, eight lines from code that already had it right.

62.9%DISPUTED CASES AGAINST THE CORPUS

Against a threshold of 20%, written down before any reviewer saw a case. The rule said that crossing it means the benchmark gets fixed, not the model. It was crossed by a factor of three.

What follows is the audit, the defect it exposed, and a measured answer to the only question that matters afterwards: did fixing the labels actually change the scores, or did two runs just differ?

01 · THE AUDITReviewers who had never seen our answer

68 cases went to Claude Sonnet 4.5, GPT‑4.1 and Llama 3.3 70B. Each was shown only the Thai transcript and the written labelling spec — no expected label, no group name, no model answer, no sight of each other. None of the three is an arm in this evaluation, so none was grading its own homework. The packet's blindness is a test, not a promise.

The 30 undisputed controls are what make the result readable. If reviewers had scored badly on those, the finding would be that they could not do the task.

02 · THE MECHANISMThe corpus contradicted itself

In business_labels.py, the product was drawn from a global mix. Eight lines below, choose_call_result already took the scenario and constrained on it. Product and scenario were independent — and the product is spoken aloud in the dialogue.

So the generator produced calls where the agent walks the customer through rebooting a router while the customer calls the service a TV box, and then graded every model against the TV box.

Two derivations, never introduced to each other

The generator's scenario constraint and the blind reviewers were worked out from opposite ends — one from the code, one from the transcripts. They produce the same corrections, down to the counts:

03 · ATTRIBUTIONIs the improvement real, or did two runs differ?

Scores went up after the fix. That is not evidence by itself. The honest test uses a property of the repair: it changed exactly one field. call_result, reason and the phone number are byte‑identical between the old and new corpus, and the audio changed on precisely the rows whose label changed — verified by per‑item SHA‑256, 138 of 138.

That splits the corpus into three cells, and the analysis refuses to run if it doesn't hold.

What the 100% means, precisely

It is not that the models got better. Those 30 calls previously had two defensible answers and were graded against one of them. Now the dialogue is self‑consistent and every arm gets them right — which also means those calls no longer discriminate between arms at all. That is the correct outcome for a defect, and worth saying plainly rather than banking as a win.

Qwen ASR is unavailable, and that is reported rather than patched

qwen3-asr-1.7b stalled on the long tail of the corpus mid‑run and stopped answering entirely; a probe against a file it had already transcribed cleanly an hour earlier timed out too, while the shared labeller answered in 0.34 s. 120 of 138 transcripts exist. Scoring the arm on 120 calls would put it behind a denominator nobody would notice had moved, so the coverage gate refuses and the arm is UNAVAILABLE — one of the plan's preregistered verdicts.

05 · WHAT THIS DOES NOT SHOWThe comparison that isn't safe to make

call_result rose sharply between the two runs — ceiling from 0.645 to 0.839. None of that can be credited to the fix, because call_result labels did not change. Something about the labeller's behaviour did:

  • on 57 calls with byte‑identical audio and identical labels, the two runs disagree on 16–20% of call_result answers;
  • within a single run, arms disagree with themselves across three replicates on 17 to 33 items of 138 — and the old run was no different (Typhoon 30 of 138). Instability is a standing property of this task, not something that changed between the runs;
  • but on the identical‑audio control the ceiling arm's call_result accuracy rose 73.2% → 91.1%, which is more than replicate noise comfortably accounts for — and the old run recorded no model identity at all, so whether the served labeller changed cannot be established after the fact.

That last point is why the parity work matters more than its individual fixes did. Every E24 call records who answered (google/gemini-2.5-flash, served by Google; qwen3.8-27b-fp8 internally). The next time this question comes up it will be answerable.

Corrections to my own earlier claims

Two rationales I gave for the parity changes did not survive checking, and both flattered the change. I said cost was never recorded — it was, $3.365 across the old run's Gemini arm; I had queried a field that does not exist. And I implied Gemini was running with reasoning enabled — reasoning tokens were already zero on all 408 calls. Of the four parity findings, two were real: a stray text fragment sent only to the audio arm, and the missing model identity.

A scoring path that does not match the preregistration

The plan says two things: every headline figure is computed on replicate 1 alone, and a parse failure is scored as incorrect, never dropped — the second existing precisely so an arm cannot improve its score by failing to answer. The scorer's collapse takes the first parseable replicate, which is a third thing: an item whose replicate 1 did not parse is scored from replicate 2.

On this run that rescues exactly three items — ASR‑120, ASR‑122, ASR‑135 — and all three belong to Gemini, the only arm with any parse failures. So the deviation moves one arm, in that arm's favour:

The scorer was not changed to produce it — rewriting an estimator after seeing which arm it favours is the failure a preregistration exists to prevent. Both numbers are reported and the discrepancy is left for a reviewed decision.

06 · PROCESSGates that were added, and one that had never fired

The old experiment's plan says every corpus hash is stamped at a freeze before the first model call. It sits at status: draft with every hash still null, after 1,002 model calls. Nobody decided to skip it — there was no command that performed it. There is now, and E24's freeze is stamped from the rendered bytes before its first call.

Two audio files failed a speech‑rate check. Rather than widen the threshold — which would disable it for all 138 files so one run could pass — the check's own premise was tested: against a 3,513‑character reference, the two ASR systems returned 3,365 and 3,377 characters. The files carry speech. They are now an enumerated exception carrying that evidence, and anything not on the list still aborts.

What none of this settles

The reviewers are models, not people. A human panel remains the thing that would settle these labels; this was worth running first because knowing the answer before convening one beats knowing it after. And RECONCILED: NO — correcting a synthetic corpus against its own written spec does not move it one step closer to a real production call.

The recommendation stands where it started

Do not change models on this evidence. The benchmark now measures what it claims to measure on one dimension it previously did not, one arm could not be run, and the dimension that moved most is the one whose movement is unexplained. Adopt Typhoon over Qwen ASR on reliability grounds — it transcribed 138 of 138 where Qwen stalled outright — and not because it wins a business‑F1 argument.
